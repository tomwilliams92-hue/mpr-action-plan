# Reading a listing

What to extract, and what it costs when it goes unnoticed. Most of these are
invisible to the arithmetic — the appraisal will happily return a confident
answer for a property that cannot legally be let.

## EPC and how it is heated

The single most consequential pairing on any listing, and the one most often
skipped because it sits in small print.

A rented property needs **E or better** to be let at all, and the direction of
travel is C for new tenancies. Look at three things together:

- **The rating itself.** "EPC: Awaiting", "Awaiting assessment" or a blank means
  there is no rating — not that it is fine. On a refurbishment project this is a
  genuine unknown, not an administrative gap.
- **The heating.** Electric heating — storage heaters, panel heaters, electric
  boilers — scores badly, because the EPC is modelled on fuel cost. It is the
  most common single cause of an F or G.
- **The walls.** Solid stone, solid brick and anything pre-1920 without a cavity
  cannot be cheaply insulated. Internal wall insulation costs thousands, eats
  floor area in a small house, and brings condensation risk if detailed badly.

Electric heating *and* solid walls *and* no published rating is the worst
combination on the board. Get the rating, or an assessor's view, before bidding.
The fix — a gas connection, a heat pump, internal insulation — can absorb a
whole cosmetic refurb budget before anyone has bought a kitchen, and an
unfixable EPC is not a cost line. It is a reason to walk away.

## Which kind of auction

Establish this first, because it changes the cost base by thousands.

**Traditional auction** — contracts exchange on the fall of the hammer,
completion in 28 days. Buyer's premium roughly £1,500–£3,000 plus an admin fee,
and the seller's legal pack costs are often passed on.

**Modern method of auction** (also "auction by conditional sale", usually run by
iamsold or Pattinson through a high street agent) — a reservation agreement, 56
days to complete. The **buyer** pays a reservation fee of around 4.5% including
VAT, subject to a minimum near £6,600. It does not come off the purchase price
and it is not refundable.

That minimum is what catches people. It is a fixed cost, so the cheaper the lot
the worse it bites — on a £62,000 purchase, £6,600 is 10.6% of the price and
roughly double the Land Transaction Tax. Always convert it to a percentage of
the actual price and put that figure in front of the user; a number expressed as
"4.5%" reads as small and behaves as enormous.

Also note the guide price, and be precise about what it means. The reserve is
**confidential** — the agent and seller know it and nobody else does, so never
state it as though it were known. What is known is the constraint: under ASA and
Property Ombudsman guidance, where a single guide figure is published the reserve
must be within 10% of it. A £70,000 guide therefore means a reserve somewhere
between £70,000 and £77,000, and it may well be at the bottom of that band.

The guide can sit below the reserve because it is a marketing figure meant to
attract bidders, not the seller's floor — which is exactly the bait-pricing
problem the 10% cap was introduced to limit. Buyers reasonably assume the guide
is a floor, so explain it rather than asserting a reserve figure.

The practical consequence: an appraisal run at the guide flatters every number in
it. If the user's maximum is below the guide, they are hoping for an unsold lot
and a post-auction approach rather than bidding, and they should know that going
in.

## Layout tells

Floor plans say things the description will not.

- **Bathroom on the wrong floor.** A shower room in a cellar or lower ground
  floor, with no facility on the bedroom floor, caps both rent and resale
  against anything conventional, and moving it is expensive — drainage, not
  decoration.
- **Rooms reached through other rooms.** A bedroom entered through another
  bedroom is not two lettable bedrooms in most valuers' eyes.
- **Bedroom sizes.** Under about 6.5 sq m and it is a box room whatever the
  particulars call it. Check the dimensions rather than the bedroom count.
- **Total floor area.** Anything under about 650 sq ft for a two-bed is small,
  and small houses have a hard ceiling on both rent and value regardless of
  specification.
- **Three storeys in a small footprint.** Often means a converted cellar with
  limited head height, damp risk and a staircase eating the floor area twice.

## Words that mean money

- "Requiring a programme of upgrading and refurbishment throughout" — full
  refurb, not cosmetic. Budget accordingly and expect to find more.
- "Investors, developers and cash buyers" — the agent expects it to be
  unmortgageable. Assume bridging and check why.
- "Some updating required" — usually cosmetic, occasionally not.
- "No onward chain" — often probate. Can mean a long-empty property with the
  damp and decay that follows, and a council tax empty premium already running.
- "Potential to extend, subject to consents" — no consents exist. Value it at
  nil until they do.

## Tenure and charges

Freehold is the simple case. For leasehold, get the term remaining, the ground
rent, the service charge and — most important — whether major works are planned.
An ex-local-authority block can carry a five-figure section 20 bill that arrives
without warning and is enough on its own to destroy the cashflow of an otherwise
sound flat. Under about 80 years remaining, factor in a lease extension.

## Where it is

- **Council tax band** tells you the holding cost while empty, and Welsh
  councils may charge a premium of up to 300% on long-term empty homes. Conwy,
  Gwynedd, Denbighshire and Pembrokeshire all apply substantial premiums. On a
  long refurb this runs to thousands.
- **Flood risk** on anything coastal or near a watercourse — check before the
  survey, because it affects insurance cost and lender appetite as well as
  value.
- **Article 4 directions** matter if the plan is ever an HMO.
- **How thin the local market is.** A village with a handful of sales a year
  gives you few comparables, a cautious valuer, and slower voids than a town.
