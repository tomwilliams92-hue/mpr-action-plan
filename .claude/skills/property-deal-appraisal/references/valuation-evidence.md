# Establishing the three numbers that decide the deal

The appraisal is arithmetic. The judgement is in the inputs. Three of them carry
almost all the risk, and all three fail in the same direction — optimistically.

## Post-refurb value

This is the most important number in a BRRR deal, because it sets the refinance
loan and therefore the capital you get back. It is also the number a surveyor
will independently test at the worst possible moment, six months in, when you
have already spent the refurb budget.

**Use sold prices, not asking prices.** Land Registry Price Paid data and the
sold sections of Rightmove and Zoopla are the evidence a valuer will use. Asking
prices tell you what sellers hope for.

**Match the comparable properly.** Same street or an adjoining one, same property
type, same number of bedrooms, similar size, similar condition after works. A
three-bed semi two streets away is not a comparable for a two-bed terrace.

**Prefer recent sales.** Within six months ideally, twelve at the outside. Adjust
for any obvious market movement since.

**Take the middle, not the top.** If comparables run £150,000 to £175,000, model
£160,000, not £175,000. The top of a range is usually the best example in the best
condition with the best garden, and a valuer instructed by a lender is
professionally cautious by disposition and by liability.

**Know the ceiling.** Most streets have a price no property on them exceeds
regardless of specification. Spending £40,000 on a house in a street where nothing
has ever sold above £150,000 does not produce a £180,000 house; it produces a very
nice £150,000 house. Check the highest sale on the street in the last three years
before assuming a refurb breaks through it.

A down-valuation is the most common way a BRRR deal fails, and it is worth saying
so explicitly to anyone who has modelled a confident end value. Run the 10%-down
sensitivity and look at what it does to the capital left in.

## Achievable rent

**Use let comparables, not listings.** A property advertised at £950 that sat for
eleven weeks and went at £875 is evidence for £875. Rightmove shows current
listings; a local letting agent will tell you what actually let and how fast.

**Ask an agent who wants the instruction.** They will give you a real figure, and
an agent's written rental assessment is what a BTL lender wants anyway. Ask for
the achievable figure and the likely time to let, not the asking figure.

**Match the specification.** Rent is more sensitive to condition, EPC, parking and
whether it is furnished than sale prices are. Compare like with like.

**Check the local housing allowance rate** for the broad rental market area. It
sets the floor under rents in lower-value areas and tells you how much of the
market is benefit-supported, which affects both void risk and lender appetite.

The rent matters twice: it drives the cashflow, and through the interest cover
ratio it can cap the refinance loan below the LTV limit. On a low-yielding
property the stress test binds and the money released is less than 75% of the
value however good the valuation is.

## Refurbishment cost

**Build it from a schedule of works, room by room.** Per-square-foot rules are a
cross-check, not an estimate. A number arrived at by multiplying floor area by a
rate is a guess wearing a suit.

**Get the property surveyed if it needs real work.** A level 3 building survey at
£800–£1,400 is cheap against a £15,000 roof discovered after completion. For
anything pre-1930, budget for the survey as a matter of course.

**Price the invisible things first.** Rewiring, replumbing, damp, roof, structural
movement and drainage are where budgets die. Kitchens and bathrooms are
predictable; what is behind the plaster is not.

**Get quotes before bidding, not after.** At auction you are committed on the
fall of the hammer, so the schedule of works and at least one builder's view need
to exist beforehand. If that is not possible, widen the contingency rather than
narrowing the estimate.

**Contingency is not padding.** 15% standard, 20–25% on older stock or anything
with known damp, roof or structural issues. The contingency is the part of the
job you have not found yet, and on a Victorian terrace you will find some.

## A note on how to use these

When a user gives round numbers — "it'll be worth about £180k, rent for £900,
maybe £25k of work" — that is three optimistic guesses multiplying together, and
the appraisal inherits all of it. Push back once, clearly, and offer to run the
downside case alongside the base case so the shape of the risk is visible.

Do not, however, refuse to run the numbers on estimates. An appraisal on rough
inputs is genuinely useful for deciding whether a property is worth a viewing and
a survey. Just be clear about which parts of the answer are load-bearing and
which would move.
